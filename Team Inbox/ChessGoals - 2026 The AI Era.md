# 2026: The AI Era

_Source: Kris's coach (ChessGoals), shared 2026-08-17 as direct input for M4's Coach-mode pedagogy pass._

Okay, what about today's climate? I believe the balance has shifted from tools being helpful and efficient into digital overload.

AI tools are everywhere now. I get an email every week from someone asking ChessGoals to partner with their new AI chess tool. Maybe some of them are cool and useful, but right now I'm not interested in that. We are already flooded with too much AI slop. Let's dive into why.

**Over-Analysis:** I don't want to call out any specific websites here, but the amount of feedback some of these sites give users based on one game is absurd. You realistically only need between one and three takeaways from each game. And if they are blitz games, one should be plenty. Then you move onto the next game. You don't need an AI coach that gives you paragraphs of text between each move. These AI coaches do not truly understand you as a player, and they definitely don't replace the value of a real coach… especially not a real coach who knows your style and uses Stockfish as a tool to guide recommendations.

**Massive Courses:** It's easier than ever now to make a 1000-line course with tens of thousands of words of text. Who. Cares. We need focus and structure to improve as busy adults. Just give us the good stuff!

## What Works Best

The positive side is we can pick and choose exactly what we want to use technology for! 2026 can be the best year for chess study!

**Engines as Guides:** If you can afford a coach, I recommend it and it has shown to help players improve in the ChessGoals Survey. Second best to a coach is using Stockfish to guide your takeaways. This combines instant correct results with your brainpower to work through why the results are correct. Learning to work with the engine is a skill in itself and a very important one for chess improvement.

**Human-Designed Repertoires:** Find curated opening systems designed by humans with computer tools to check their work. ChessGoals opening courses are usually 100 lines, based on games of players at your level (thanks to online databases), and have no errors (thanks to chess engines). These are super efficient, fun, and keep you focused on playing games and improving.

**Active Learning:** When it comes to correcting weaknesses, I prefer to keep a Google Sheet with my own takeaways. The work of manually doing this I think helps me cement the items I'm actively working on in each game. This is more efficient than pen and paper, but not by much. Pen and paper is a great way to do this as well. Don't just passively scroll through AI-generated suggestions.
